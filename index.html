Deploy The Strategy

Why this campaign exists

Who Is This For

The bullseye is narrow on purpose: independent voters aged 18 to 34. Independent is overwhelmingly a young person’s registration. Nationally 47% of adults 18 to 29 identify as independents, more than any generation before them at the same age, and 57% of independents 18 to 34 have never been registered with either party at all. They are not lapsed Democrats or Republicans. They started outside and stayed there.

That is the group this campaign is built for. They carry no party telling them how to vote, they were shut out of the closed primary, they are the least likely to have voted before, and they are the cheapest people in the city to reach because they already live on the platforms this plan runs on. No other campaign in this race is aimed at them.

Around that center the same work reaches the rest of the city: older independents, working families, longtime Washingtonians, renters, small-business owners, and residents who follow D.C. news and culture on social media. They are the spillover, not the target.

Independent D.C. voters

91,000

About 18% of the city’s registered electorate.

This is not a small niche. It is a citywide audience larger than D.C.’s Republican registration base, and large enough to matter across wards, neighborhoods, and community conversations.

Why This Works

Four Numbers Behind The Bet

  • 80% of adults 18 to 29 use Instagram, and 63% use TikTok. Among adults 65 and over it is 19% and 12%. Reaching this voter through mail or television means paying to reach everybody else first. Reaching them on a phone is the only efficient route there is.
  • 9% is the average turnout for voters 18 to 34 in big-city mayoral races. New York in 2013 came in at 8%. Boston in 2015 was under 2% of voters 18 to 29. This group does not skip elections because it is hard to reach. It skips them because nobody runs at it.
  • 28% is what youth turnout hit in the 2025 New York mayoral election. From single digits to 28% in one cycle, because one campaign was built for young voters instead of treating them as a bonus. That is the proof this is not theory.
  • 17.2% of registered D.C. voters carry no party, and they are concentrated, not scattered. Ward 2 sits at 20.90% and Ward 3 at 19.05%, and the ten heaviest precincts in the city run through the university corridors. A small budget aimed at those blocks behaves like a much larger one aimed everywhere.

Every other campaign in this race is spending to persuade people who already vote. Those voters are expensive, they are contested by everyone, and most of them decided months ago. The people in the four numbers above cost less, hear from nobody, and have just been shown to move when somebody finally speaks to them.

Platform use: Pew Research Center, Americans’ Social Media Use, fielded February to June 2025. Mayoral youth turnout: CIRCLE at Tufts University. Registration shares: D.C. Board of Elections monthly report, as of August 31, 2026.

The Saturation Audit

Where The Money Goes

Break The List Down By Ward

Spreading a citywide budget evenly across D.C. is how a small budget disappears. Independents are not scattered at random and neither is the turnout gap. So the first job is taking the voting list, breaking it down by ward, and putting the weight where the numbers are.

  • ✓ The list, split eight ways. The Board of Elections publishes the registration numbers by ward every month, with a no-party column. We break the voting list down across the eight wards and count what is actually in each one.
  • ✓ Rank the wards twice, for two different jobs. Once by the share of voters registered with no party, once by how far general-election turnout has fallen below the city. Those two lists are not the same list, and they do not get the same message.
  • ✓ Target one: where the independents are thickest. The wards and neighborhoods carrying the heaviest no-party registration get the persuasion work, with everything inside them weighted to residents 18 to 34.
  • ✓ Target two: new voters in the Black neighborhoods that sat out. The audit already found more than 40% of voters east of the Anacostia undecided before the primary, in the Black wards where general-election turnout runs lowest. Those residents get the registration and turnout work, not the persuasion work. Nobody is fighting for them, which makes them the cheapest votes in the city and the ones most worth earning.
  • ✓ Ads follow the map, then narrow by age. Delivery is concentrated on the top wards instead of the whole District, and inside them it is set to 18 to 34. A political ad buy cannot be drawn to a ward or a ZIP code, and the smallest radius the platform allows on political is fifteen miles, which covers the whole District and then some. So geography alone will not find these residents. The ward map decides where the crew walks and where the message is written from, and the targeting below does the rest.
  • ✓ The two cuts point at the same people. The District does not publish a table crossing age with party, so the exact D.C. count is not something anyone can quote. It does not need to be. The no-party wards and the 18 to 34 band overlap heavily by the nature of who registers independent, so the ward map finds the geography and the age setting finds the people inside it.
  • ✓ Posters follow the same map. The street crew works a routed list of blocks inside those same wards, weighted to where younger residents actually are: transit stops, campuses and the corridors where they eat, shop and go out. A resident sees the poster on their corner and the video in their feed in the same week, and that repetition is what makes a name stick.

The audit is delivered as the eight wards ranked, with the ad geographies and the poster route already built from it, so every dollar after that is aimed.

How the ad budget gets set

Meta charges by CPM, the price of 1,000 impressions. Nobody can tell you this campaign’s CPM before it runs. It moves with the audience, the creative and whoever else is bidding for the same feed that week, and published averages are somebody else’s campaign, not this one. The first week of spending establishes the real number. A person who sees the ad three times is three impressions, all of them paid for, so reach and frequency trade against each other on whatever the budget is.

Reach × frequency = impressions. Impressions × CPM = budget

Start from the number of residents you want to reach, decide how many times each one should see the ad, and that gives the impressions you have to buy. Multiply those by the rate and you have the budget. Worked below at a $12 CPM purely to show the shape of it, showing the ad twice to each person. That rate is an assumption, not a quote, and it gets replaced with the measured number after the first week:

  • 150,000 residents, twice each300,000 impressions · $3,600
  • 250,000 residents, twice each500,000 impressions · $6,000
  • 500,000 residents, twice each1,000,000 impressions · $12,000
  • 1,000,000 residents, twice each2,000,000 impressions · $24,000

Once the test returns a real CPM, the same arithmetic says exactly what the $3,200 to $5,000 in this plan buys, and we bring you that number instead of a forecast. What is certain either way: raising the frequency to three views buys the same money fewer people, because every extra view is another impression to pay for.

Anyone who quotes you a reach figure before the first dollar is spent is guessing, and a big reach paired with a high frequency on a small budget is a guess that the arithmetic above does not support. We would rather show you the method now than a report that misses later.

What the posters say

One message per poster. A poster is read at fifteen feet by someone walking past, so it carries one line, one instruction and a code big enough to scan from the sidewalk. Two versions, printed to match the two kinds of block on the route.

  • ✓ On the independent blocks: “You were locked out in June. November 3 is open.” The closed primary shut these voters out and many still think it applies. The poster corrects it in one line and the code takes them to a page that confirms they are registered.
  • ✓ On the low-turnout blocks: “A ballot is coming to this address. Don’t throw it out.” In a neighborhood that has not voted in years a government envelope gets binned. Naming it before it arrives is the whole job, and the code opens the drop-box map.
  • ✓ The issue line, from the polling, not from us. Cost of living is the top concern in the District’s own survey data, ahead of safety and housing. A rotating second run carries that as a question rather than a promise, so the poster sounds like the block it is on.
  • ✓ Every poster carries the same three marks. The date it went up, which the law requires, the paid-for attribution, and the code. No poster leaves the print run without all three.

Posted legal, and taken back down. D.C. limits posters to three per side of a street within a block, six per block when both sides are used, which means 500 posters need 91 block-slots once each precinct allocation respects that ceiling and 1,000 need 172. Every campaign poster must then come down within thirty days of the election, which is December 3, or the campaign is fined per sign left standing.
